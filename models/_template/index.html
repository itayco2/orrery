Why does a turning wheel make a wave?

Watch one point on a steadily turning wheel and follow only its height. What you get is the most important curve in physics.

This page is Orrery’s exhibit kit: a small, complete example that new exhibits start from. It is not listed among the exhibits.

Take a wheel turning at a steady speed and paint a dot on its rim. Now forget the sideways motion and watch only how high the dot is. It rises, slows near the top, falls, slows near the bottom, and rises again. Plot that height against time and you get a smooth, endlessly repeating curve: a sine wave.

In the figure below, the wheel is on the left and the height of its dot is drawn out to the right as time passes. Change the speed and watch the wave bunch up or stretch out.

Why a sine wave? The height of the dot is the radius times the sine of the angle the wheel has turned through. Turning at a steady rate means the angle grows steadily with time, so:

height = r · sin(2πft)

where r is the radius, f the number of turns per second and t the time in seconds. The same curve describes a weight bobbing on a spring. That is no coincidence: the shadow of a steadily turning peg moves in exactly the way physicists call simple harmonic motion.

Try this

In the next figure, drag the dot around the circle — or click the circle and use the arrow keys. Watch the two numbers: the dot’s height is the sine of the angle, and its sideways position is the cosine.

Note

Angles here are measured anticlockwise from the rightward direction, as mathematicians do.

What this model leaves out

  • A real wheel never turns at a perfectly steady speed; friction slows it and whatever drives it speeds it up. Any change in speed distorts the wave.
  • Real springs and pendulums lose energy, so their swings shrink over time (damping). A pendulum follows a sine wave closely only for small swings.

Astronomy

Where are the planets tonight?

Right now each planet is at one particular place on its orbit, and you can work out where. Turn the crank of this model to see where they are tonight, why they seem to wander back and forth among the stars, and why the outer ones hardly move at all.

An explorable model · positions computed in your browser from NASA JPL’s orbital elements

Watch the night sky for a few weeks and most of the stars keep their places: the patterns of the constellations never change. A handful of bright points do not. They drift slowly through the constellations, usually eastward, but every so often one stops, backs up for a few weeks and then carries on again. The Greeks called them planētai, “wanderers”, which is where the word planet comes from.

The backwards bit is called retrograde motion. For well over a thousand years astronomers explained it with circles riding on circles. It is tempting to think the planet really does turn round. It doesn’t. Every planet goes steadily round the Sun in the same direction, all the time. The loops come from where we watch: we are riding on a planet that is moving too.

From above: the solar system today

Each planet moves on an ellipse, a slightly squashed circle, with the Sun at one focus. Six numbers, the orbital elements, pin down that ellipse and where the planet is on it at a given moment. They say how big the orbit is, how squashed, how it is tilted and turned, and where the planet was at a reference date. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory publishes a set of these numbers for each planet, with small yearly drifts, fitted so they stay accurate from the year 1800 to 2050. From them, a page of arithmetic gives each planet’s position on any date. This page does that arithmetic every time you move the slider.

Distances in the solar system are measured in astronomical units (au). One au is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 149.6 million km. Directions are measured as an angle around the Sun called ecliptic longitude, counted anticlockwise (seen from above the North Pole) from the direction of the Sun at the March equinox.

The figure opens at today’s date, from your computer’s clock. Press Play and watch Mercury race round while Neptune barely creeps. Drag round the Sun to turn time yourself: one full turn is one year. Then switch the scale to true distances and see how empty the outer solar system is.

The last column of the table answers the question in the title. A planet that sits east of the Sun in our sky follows the Sun down in the west after sunset: it is an evening object. One west of the Sun rises before it: a morning object. The angle between a planet and the Sun, seen from Earth, is called its elongation. When it is less than about 15°, the planet is lost in the Sun’s glare. When it is near 180°, the planet is opposite the Sun: it rises at sunset, is highest at midnight and is up all night. That position is called opposition, and it is when an outer planet is closest and brightest.

At the end of September 2026, when this page was written, Saturn was a few days from opposition (4 October 2026), Neptune had just passed its own (26 September), and Venus was low in the evening twilight, catching up on Earth before passing between us and the Sun on 24 October. Mars and Jupiter were morning objects. The figure recomputes all of this for whatever date it shows.

The view from Earth

What we see from Earth is only a direction: the line from Earth to the planet, projected onto the far-off stars. Both ends of that line move. Earth goes round the Sun once a year; Mars takes 687 days. Most of the time both motions carry the line of sight eastward, and Mars drifts east among the stars. But roughly every 26 months Earth, on its faster inside track, overtakes Mars. For a few weeks around that moment the line of sight swings backwards, just as a slower car you are passing on a motorway seems to slide backwards against the distant hills.

Try this

Press Play and watch the dashed line from Earth to Mars. On the right, the planet’s direction is plotted against time; the stretch where it runs backwards is drawn thick. Press Next opposition and notice that the backward stretch is always centred on the moment Earth passes between the Sun and the planet. Then try Jupiter and Saturn: their loops are smaller and come about once a year.

The next time this happens for Mars is early 2027. The model has Mars stop and turn backwards on 10 January 2027, reach opposition on 19 February, come closest to Earth the next day at 0.678 au (101 million km), and turn forward again on 1 April. Published predictions give the same dates, and the same distance to within about ten thousand kilometres (JPL Horizons: 0.6779 au). It is a distant opposition: Mars will be near the far point of its orbit. In August 2003, when Earth overtook Mars near its near point, the gap was only 55.8 million km.

Mercury and Venus loop too, but for them the roles are swapped: they are on the inside track and overtake us. Their backward stretches happen around inferior conjunction, when they pass between Earth and the Sun and are lost in its glare.

Why the outer planets are slow

Saturn’s oppositions come round only about 13 days later each year. They fell on 8 September 2024, 21 September 2025 and 4 October 2026. That is because Saturn itself hardly moves; Earth is doing nearly all of the work. Saturn takes 29.4 years to go round once.

Two things make a distant planet’s year long. Its path is longer, in proportion to its distance. And it moves more slowly, because the Sun’s pull is weaker out there: Earth travels at 29.8 km/s, Neptune at only 5.4 km/s. Johannes Kepler found the combined rule in 1619. Measure the orbit’s size a (half its longest width) in au and the period T in years, and

T2 = a3

In words: square the year and you get the cube of the distance. A planet four times farther out than Earth takes eight years, not four. On logarithmic axes, where each step along an axis multiplies the value by ten, this rule becomes a straight line.

The last readout ties the figures together. Earth laps a planet whose year is T every 1 ÷ (1 − 1/T) years. For Mars (T = 1.88) that is 2.1 years, or 780 days, so Mars loops only every other year or so. For Saturn it is 1.035 years (378 days): a slow planet is lapped almost exactly once a year, which is why its opposition moves only about two weeks later each year. And the farther out a planet is, the smaller its loop, because Earth’s orbit looks smaller from there.

So, to find a planet tonight: find its direction from the Sun in Figure 1, and remember that the Sun sets in the west. Evening planets are in the western sky after sunset, a planet near opposition rises in the east as the Sun sets, and morning planets wait for the hours before dawn.

What this model leaves out

  • Flat drawing. The orbits are tilted up to 7° (Mercury) from Earth’s, but the figures draw everything in one plane. Distances and the angle from the Sun are computed in three dimensions; directions in Figure 2 are ecliptic longitudes only, so the up-and-down part of a real loop, which makes it a loop or a zigzag rather than a back-and-forth, is not shown.
  • Approximate positions. JPL’s simple elements are fitted to its full ephemeris for 1800–2050 only, so the time slider stops there. JPL gives their errors as up to about 40″ (arcseconds) for Mars and up to 10′ for Saturn; the dates in the text agree with published predictions to within a few hours. “Earth” is really the centre of mass of Earth and Moon.
  • No light-time, aberration or precession. We see each planet where it was when its light left it (4 hours ago for Neptune), and longitudes here are measured from the equinox of the year 2000, which has since slid by about a third of a degree.
  • Sizes are not to scale. At true scale even Jupiter would be far smaller than a pixel.
  • Visibility is rough. The 15° glare limit is a rule of thumb. Whether you can actually see a planet also depends on its brightness, your latitude, the time of year, the horizon and the weather. The table uses the date at noon UTC, not your local evening.
  • Dwarf planets, moons and the planets’ own gravity on each other are left out, except as they are built into JPL’s fitted drift rates.
